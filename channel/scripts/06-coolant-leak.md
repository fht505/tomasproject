## COLD OPEN

[broll: hand pulling the coolant reservoir cap area into view, low tank against the MIN line]

Every few weeks, the same ritual: the low-coolant light blinks on, you
top up the tank, and everything's fine again. For a while. And each
time, a little voice asks — *where is it going?*

Here's the uncomfortable rule from last episode, worth repeating because
it drives this entire hour: **coolant does not evaporate.** The system is
sealed. A dropping level is coolant leaving — into your driveway, into
another part of the car, or out the tailpipe as steam. Tonight we find
which, with a flashlight, a piece of cardboard, and about ten minutes of
actual effort.

[callout: channel card — "Why Is My Car Doing That?"]

## THE SYMPTOM, PRECISELY

[callout: three-way split — "Puddle you can see" / "Level drops, no puddle" / "Smell or fog inside the car"]

Three versions of this problem, three different hunts.

**There's a puddle.** Congratulations — external leak, the easy hunt.
Coolant is dyed (green, orange, pink, blue) and smells faintly sweet.
Clear odorless water near the front passenger area on a hot day is
usually AC condensation — that one's normal and free.

**Level drops, no puddle, ever.** The sneaky tier: coolant leaving
somewhere you can't see — while driving, onto hot parts that flash it
to steam, or *inside* the engine. That's where the pressure test and
the previous two episodes come in.

**You smell it inside the car** — sweet smell from the vents, an oily
film fogging the windshield, damp passenger-side carpet: that's the
heater core, the radiator's little cousin living inside your dash.

## THE CARDBOARD TEST — FREE DIAGNOSIS OVERNIGHT

[flow: car parked over a large flattened cardboard box overnight; morning: drip stains mapped to zones — front/center/rear of engine bay]

Before any shop visit: park overnight over a big flattened cardboard
box. Morning gives you a map — WHAT color landed WHERE. Coolant color
at the very front: radiator territory. Mid-engine, driver or passenger
side: hoses or water pump. A stain that's engine-oil brown is a
different episode. No stains at all plus a dropping level: internal or
under-pressure-only leak — skip straight to the pressure-test section.
Bring the cardboard story (or a photo) to your shop and you've cut real
diagnostic time. [TECH CONFIRM — do customers who bring driveway-map
info actually save diag money at FHT, or does the shop redo it anyway?]

## LEAK SITE #1 — HOSES AND CLAMPS (most common)

[cutaway: cooling loop from ep 5 — hose runs highlighted; closeup on a hose end swollen and seeping at the clamp]

Rubber lives a hard life next to an engine: heat cycles, pressure
cycles, ozone. Hoses fail at the ends first — swelling, hairline
splits, seepage at clamps that leaves crusty dried-coolant trails
(white, green, or rusty residue you can spot with a flashlight). Squeeze
test on a COLD engine: a healthy hose is firm and springy; a dying one
is either rock-hard and crackly or soft and mushy. [TECH CONFIRM — bless
the squeeze-test description; anything you check that beginners can too?]

## LEAK SITE #2 — THE RADIATOR

[cutaway: radiator — plastic end tanks, crimp seams, fin-field; seep points glowing at tank seams]

Modern radiators are aluminum cores with **plastic end tanks** crimped
on — and those tanks and crimp seams are where age shows: hairline
cracks, seep-then-split behavior, crusty residue trails. Road debris
punctures happen too. Small radiator leaks love to seal themselves with
grime for weeks, then let go on a road trip. [TECH CONFIRM — plastic
end-tank cracking: fair to call it THE radiator failure mode you see?]

## LEAK SITE #3 — WATER PUMP WEEP HOLE

[cutaway: pump from ep 5 — weep hole underneath, slow drip, dried trail down the block]

Last episode's tell-tale, now in the hunt: pumps announce their seal is
dying through a designed **weep hole**. The drip often only runs when
the engine's hot and pressurized — so the cardboard catches it but your
casual glance doesn't. Dried streaks running DOWN from the pump area
are the fingerprint. A weeping pump is a *scheduled* replacement, not an
emergency — but scheduled means soon. [TECH CONFIRM — your typical
weep-to-failure runway estimate]

## LEAK SITE #4 — THE HEATER CORE (the one inside the car)

[cutaway: dash cross-section — heater core behind the dashboard, sweet fog path to windshield, drip path to passenger carpet]

The heater core is a small radiator inside the dash. When it leaks, the
evidence is interior: sweet smell from the vents, oily film on the
inside of the windshield that keeps coming back, damp or sweet-smelling
passenger-front carpet. This is the leak people chase under the hood
for months while it's been raining inside the dashboard. Repair honesty:
the part is cheap and the labor is famous — on many cars the whole dash
comes out. [TECH CONFIRM — dash-out labor hours range you quote; any
cars where it's easy?]

## LEAK SITE #5 — INTERNAL: THE PRESSURE TEST TIER

[cutaway: pressure tester pumping the system; gauge holding vs falling; cutaway showing coolant seeping into a cylinder]

No puddle anywhere, cardboard's clean, level keeps dropping: the coolant
is leaving *inside*. A shop **pressure test** pumps the cooled system to
operating pressure and watches the gauge: holds = look again outside
under pressure; falls with nothing visible = internal path. From there
it's the white-smoke episode's territory — block test, combustion gases
in the coolant, head gasket conversation. The good news: the test
itself is quick and cheap compared to what it rules in or out.

## THE REPAIR — WHAT THE MONEY LOOKS LIKE

[flow: decision tree — leak site in, job out]

Ranked by wallet impact, roughly: a hose is parts-cheap and quick; a
radiator is a mid-size job; a pump varies wildly by engine (some are
front-and-center, some live behind the timing cover and the labor IS
the job); the heater core is cheap-part-heavy-labor; internal leaks are
last episode's head-gasket math.

## URGENT OR IGNORABLE?

[callout: three-tier verdict card — "Monitor weekly" / "This week" / "Stop driving"]

**Monitor weekly:** level dropped once, no repeat, no smell, no stains.
Mark the tank, watch it. Systems do burp air pockets after service.

**This week:** any *repeating* drop, any stain on the cardboard, sweet
smell anywhere, weep trails. Leaks only grow, and the failure mode of
"small leak" is "sudden big leak far from home."

**Stop driving:** level dropping fast, temp gauge involved, or the
low-coolant light mid-drive. That's the overheating episode's fifteen-
minute drill starting NOW — a car driven dry of coolant is an engine
obituary.

## OUTRO

So: coolant never just leaves — it goes somewhere, and the somewhere is
findable. The cardboard maps it, the flashlight finds the crusty trails,
the squeeze test reads the hoses, the sweet fog names the heater core,
and the pressure test settles the invisible cases. Sealed system,
solvable mystery.
