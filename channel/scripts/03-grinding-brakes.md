## COLD OPEN

[broll: car rolling to a stop at an intersection, exterior close on front wheel]

There are car noises you can think about for a week. There are noises you
should handle this month. And then there's this one — the low, granular
grinding when you press the brake pedal, like stepping on gravel that
someone installed inside your wheel.

Grinding is the one brake noise that is almost never innocent, because of
what's physically making it: in most cases, by the time you hear a grind,
something that was supposed to be there... isn't anymore. Ten minutes,
and you'll know exactly what wore away, what it's costing you per mile,
and the one version of this noise that's actually harmless.

[callout: channel card — "Why Is My Car Doing That?"]

## THE SYMPTOM, PRECISELY

[callout: three-way split — "Grinds ONLY while braking" / "Grinds while rolling, brake or not" / "Grinds on the first stops of the day"]

Pin down when the grind happens, because the three patterns have three
different bank accounts attached.

**Only while braking** — pedal down, grind starts; pedal up, grind stops.
That's the classic, and the most urgent. It usually means friction
material is *gone*.

**While rolling, whether or not you brake** — a rhythmic grind or growl
tied to wheel speed, maybe changing when you swerve gently. That's
usually not brakes at all — that's a wheel bearing, and it gets its own
episode. We'll cover the quick tell later.

**First stops of the morning only** — a soft grind or scrape for the
first half-mile that fades. Same overnight surface rust from our squeak
episode, being scrubbed off the rotors. This is the harmless one.

## THE MECHANISM — WHAT A GRIND PHYSICALLY IS

[cutaway: pad side-view from ep 2 — friction material worn to nothing, steel backing plate contacting the rotor face]

A brake pad is a steel **backing plate** carrying a thick block of
**friction material**. That material is the part designed to touch the
rotor. It wears down over tens of thousands of miles — through the
squealing wear-indicator stage we covered last episode — and if the
squeal gets ignored long enough, the friction material wears through
completely.

Now the steel backing plate presses directly against the steel rotor.

[flow: rotor strip from ep 1 — backing plate gouging the surface, sparks of material, grooves deepening pass after pass]

Steel grinding steel is exactly what it sounds like. And it cuts both
ways: every stop machines grooves into the rotor face, turning a smooth
disc into a vinyl record. Braking performance drops — steel-on-steel has
a fraction of the friction your pads had [TECH CONFIRM — comfortable
stating "stopping distances grow noticeably," or want a stronger/softer
phrasing?] — and the rotor, which might have survived one more pad
change, becomes scrap metal cutting itself deeper every mile.

That's why grinding is the expensive noise: **the longer it runs, the
bigger the parts list.**

## CAUSE #1 — PADS WORN TO THE BACKING PLATE (the usual suspect)

[cutaway: pad wear timeline — new pad, worn pad with squealer touching, pad gone with plate contact, each stage dated]

The wear-out story has a schedule. New pads. Tens of thousands of miles
of quiet service. Then the wear-indicator squeal — the built-in
appointment reminder — for a while [TECH CONFIRM — restate your
squeal-to-grind runway estimate from ep 2 so the numbers agree]. Then
the squeal *changes character*: lower, rougher, granular. That change is
the moment friction material ran out.

One detail worth knowing: pads on the same axle rarely wear identically.
A grinding *left* front with a surviving *right* front is common — which
is why the fix is always per-axle, never per-wheel.

## CAUSE #2 — A STONE OR DEBRIS BETWEEN PAD AND ROTOR

[cutaway: caliper area with a small stone lodged between rotor and splash shield, scraping ring drawn on rotor]

Sometimes the grind arrives *suddenly*, on a car with healthy pads —
often right after gravel, a dirt road, or roadwork. A pebble kicked up
into the caliper area can lodge between the rotor and the **splash
shield** (the thin metal plate behind the disc), scraping a bright ring
into the rotor with a sound completely out of proportion to its size.

The tell: it appears out of nowhere, sometimes grinds even *without*
braking, and can vanish as suddenly as it came if the stone dislodges.
Caught early it's the cheapest fix in this episode — sometimes zero
parts, just removal. [TECH CONFIRM — how often do you see debris grinds
vs worn-pad grinds? And is bending the splash shield back the usual
resolution?]

## CAUSE #3 — RUSTED OR SCORED ROTORS

[cutaway: rotor face with deep grooves and rust pitting, pad riding the ridges]

A car that sat for weeks — airport lot, storage, a long trip — grows more
than surface rust. Deep pitting takes hold, and the first hundred miles
of driving can sound genuinely alarming as pads try to re-seat against a
cratered surface. Mild cases scrub clean and go quiet. Deep pitting
doesn't: the pad rides the high spots, the grind persists, and the rotor
needs machining or replacement. [TECH CONFIRM — your rule of thumb for
"sat too long, rotors won't recover" — weeks? months? climate-dependent?]

## CAUSE #4 — IT'S NOT THE BRAKES: THE WHEEL BEARING FAKE-OUT

[cutaway: hub assembly from ep 1 — bearing races highlighted, grinding waves drawn from hub, not pad contact]

The impostor. A failing wheel bearing grinds, growls, or hums — but tied
to **wheel speed, not brake pedal**. Quick roadside test, safe version:
find an empty stretch, gentle S-weave at low speed. Bearing noise
typically *changes* as weight shifts onto and off the bad wheel — often
quieter turning one way, louder the other. Brake-pad grinding doesn't
care about the weave; it cares about the pedal. [TECH CONFIRM — bless
the S-weave description as the safe DIY tell, or give me your preferred
version]

If your grind fails the pedal test and passes the weave test — stop
guessing, that's a bearing, and a failing bearing is a
today-not-next-week problem. Full episode on it later in the season.

## THE REPAIR — WHAT THIS COSTS, HONESTLY

[flow: repair sequence from ep 1 — wheel off, caliper hung, measurement, new parts on]

Here's the part nobody enjoys hearing, delivered straight. Once a grind
has run for a while, "just pads" is usually off the menu:

- **Caught at first grind:** pads plus rotors on that axle — the rotors
  are scored and heat-cycled; resurfacing thin modern rotors is often a
  false economy. [TECH CONFIRM — your machine-vs-replace policy, same
  answer as ep 1]
- **Ignored for weeks:** add the possibility of cooked calipers (all
  that steel-on-steel heat has to go somewhere) and, worst case,
  heat-checked hubs.
- **The debris case:** removal, maybe a splash-shield adjustment, and
  an inspection to confirm no scoring — the happy ending version.

And the math that makes the urgency real: the difference between fixing
at *first squeal* versus *after a month of grinding* is often the entire
cost of the rotors and sometimes a caliper — parts that ignoring the
appointment reminder bought you. The soundtrack was the invoice growing.

## URGENT OR IGNORABLE?

[callout: three-tier verdict card — "Scrub it off" / "Days, not weeks" / "Park it"]

**Scrub it off:** morning-only surface-rust grind that fades within a
mile. Fine. Monitor that it *always* fades.

**Days, not weeks:** any grind that happens every time you brake. You're
machining your own rotors at every stop light. It will not improve on
its own, and the parts list grows with the odometer.

**Park it:** grinding *plus* a soft or sinking pedal, grinding *plus*
the car pulling hard under braking, or bearing-grind that's gotten loud.
Those combinations mean the system's ability to stop the car is actively
degrading. Tow money is cheaper than crash money.

## OUTRO

So: a grind that lives on the brake pedal is metal that ran out of
material — pads done, rotors on the clock. A sudden grind on healthy
brakes is probably a stone in the works. A grind that rolls with the
wheels and shifts when you weave is a bearing wearing a brake costume.
And the only free grind is the morning rust-scrub that fades in a mile.

Your brakes gave you two warnings before this — the squeal, then the
grind. Next episode, we go back to the beginning of that story: the
shake in the steering wheel that starts it all.
