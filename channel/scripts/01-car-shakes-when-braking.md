## THE SYMPTOM, PRECISELY

First, let's pin down what you're feeling, because *where* the shake shows up
is the biggest diagnostic clue you have — and it's free.

[callout: three-way split — "Steering wheel shakes" / "Brake pedal pulses" / "Whole car / seat shudders"]

If the shake comes through the **steering wheel**, the problem is almost
certainly at the **front** of the car. The front wheels are connected to your
hands through the steering system — the rears aren't.

If you feel a **pulsing in the brake pedal itself** — a rhythmic push-back
against your foot — that's the braking system talking directly to you, front
or rear.

And if the **whole car shudders**, seat included, but the wheel stays fairly
calm — look to the **rear** brakes or something that isn't a brake at all.
We'll get there.

One more distinction, and it matters: does it shake **only when you're
braking**? If your car also vibrates cruising at highway speed with your foot
off the pedal, that's a different video — that's wheel balance or tires.
Today is strictly: smooth until you brake, shaking while you brake.

[callout: "Shakes ONLY while braking → this video. Shakes at speed anyway → wheel balance."]

## THE MECHANISM — WHAT'S ACTUALLY HAPPENING

Here's the ten-second version of how disc brakes work, because the whole
mystery lives in one part.

[cutaway: front corner assembly — wheel ghosted, rotor, caliper, pads labeled]

Behind each wheel is a steel disc called a **rotor** that spins with the
wheel. Straddling it is a **caliper** holding two **brake pads**. Press the
pedal, and hydraulic pressure squeezes the pads against the spinning rotor.
Friction slows the rotor; the rotor slows the wheel; the wheel slows you.

For that to feel smooth, the rotor has to be smooth — and more than smooth,
it has to be the **same thickness at every point on the disc**.

[flow: rotor spinning between pads; one exaggerated thick spot passes through the pads once per revolution, kicking them apart]

Now imagine one patch of the rotor is a few hundredths of a millimeter
thicker than the rest. Every single revolution, that thick spot forces the
pads apart and snaps them back. At 60 miles an hour your wheels turn about
**13 times per second** — so that tiny bump becomes a 13-times-a-second
hammer, straight up the brake lines into your pedal, and through the
suspension into your steering wheel.

That thickness problem has a name — **disc thickness variation** — and it's
behind most brake-shake on the road. The question is what caused it. Here are
the six causes, ranked from most to least likely.

## CAUSE #1 — UNEVEN PAD DEPOSITS ON THE ROTOR (most likely)

[cutaway: rotor surface microscopic view — smeared layer of pad material building up in one patch]

Mechanics call rotors "warped" — you've probably heard it. Here's the secret:
the steel almost never actually bends. What usually happens is that brake pad
material **transfers onto the rotor face unevenly** — a smeared, baked-on
patch of friction material that acts exactly like a thick spot, because it is
one.

The classic recipe: a long hard stop — say, a downhill or a panic brake —
that gets everything extremely hot, and then the car **sits still with the
pads clamped** on the glowing rotor. That parked clamp prints a patch of pad
material onto the disc like a branding iron. [TECH CONFIRM — is this the
leading cause you actually see in the bay, or do you see more of #2?]

[broll: glowing brake rotor on a test rig]

## CAUSE #2 — RUST AND RUNOUT

[cutaway: rotor hat face against wheel hub, exaggerated rust flakes between the two surfaces tilting the rotor slightly]

Rotor number two problem: the rotor doesn't sit perfectly square on the hub.
A film of rust between hub and rotor — or lug nuts tightened unevenly, or in
the wrong order — tilts the disc a fraction of a degree. A tilted rotor
wobbles side to side as it spins; the pads shave it thinner at two opposite
points; and a few thousand miles later you've *ground in* a thickness
variation. This one's sneaky because the shake shows up long after the cause.

Cars that sit for weeks, and cars in salt states, earn this one. [TECH
CONFIRM — how often do you trace shake to hub rust/lug torque vs. deposits?]

## CAUSE #3 — A STICKING CALIPER

[flow: caliper cross-section; slide pins seized, one pad dragging against the rotor continuously]

The caliper is supposed to squeeze and release. Its slide pins let it float
freely; when those pins corrode and seize, one pad can **drag against the
rotor all the time** — overheating one side, laying down deposits, and
cooking the rotor unevenly. Telltales: shake plus a **burning smell** near
one wheel, a car that **pulls to one side**, or one wheel noticeably hotter
than its twin after a normal drive. [TECH CONFIRM — hot-wheel check: safe to
tell viewers to hover a hand near the wheel, never touch?]

## CAUSE #4 — IT'S THE REAR BRAKES

[cutaway: full car underside, rear axle highlighted; shake waves drawn into the seat, not the wheel]

Everything so far applies to the rear brakes too — same physics. The
difference is how it feels: rear brake shake comes up through the **chassis
and your seat**, with the steering wheel mostly innocent. On cars with rear
drums, an **out-of-round drum** does the same job as a thick rotor spot.

## CAUSE #5 — WORN SUSPENSION AMPLIFYING A SMALL PROBLEM

[cutaway: control arm bushing and tie rod end, worn rubber highlighted, arrows showing slop under brake load]

Braking throws the car's weight forward and loads every suspension joint.
Worn **control-arm bushings**, **tie-rod ends**, or **ball joints** let
components shift under that load — so a rotor imperfection too small to feel
in a tight car becomes a wheel-shaking shimmy in a loose one. Clue: the shake
is worse at *higher* braking speeds and the steering also feels vague or
wandery in daily driving. [TECH CONFIRM — ranking position: do you see this
more or less often than sticking calipers?]

## CAUSE #6 — A FAILING WHEEL BEARING (least likely, most serious)

[cutaway: hub assembly, bearing races highlighted]

Rare for brake-only shake, but worth knowing: a worn wheel bearing lets the
whole hub — rotor included — wobble. Usually it announces itself with a
**hum or growl that changes when you swerve** gently side to side. If shake
comes with that soundtrack, this stops being a comfort problem. Bearings
carry the wheel. Get it checked now, not next month.

## WHAT THE REPAIR ACTUALLY INVOLVES

[flow: repair sequence — wheel off, caliper hung, rotor measured with a micrometer, new rotor + pads on, torque wrench star pattern]

Here's what a shop does — and this is worth knowing even if you never touch
a wrench, because it tells you whether you're being quoted the right job.

A good tech doesn't guess. They **measure the rotor** with a micrometer at
several points around the disc — thickness variation shows up in the
numbers. The usual fix is **new rotors and pads on that axle**, always
replaced in pairs so both sides brake evenly. Machining rotors flat used to
be standard; on many modern cars rotors are light enough that replacement
is standard practice instead. [TECH CONFIRM — your shop's
machine-vs-replace policy]

And one thing a good shop always does that a rushed one skips: **clean the
hub face and torque the lug nuts in a star pattern to spec** — because
skipping that is how cause #2 gets built into your brand-new rotors on day
one.

## URGENT OR IGNORABLE?

[callout: three-tier verdict card — "Plan it" / "This week" / "Now"]

Honest answer, tiered:

**Plan it.** Mild shake, brakes still stop straight and strong: you're not
in danger today. But deposits and runout are progressive — it will get
worse, and worn-uneven rotors eat new pads. Book it within the month.

**This week.** Shake plus pulling to one side, burning smell, or a hot
wheel: that's caliper territory — it's cooking parts and costing fuel while
it fails.

**Now.** Shake plus grinding or growling noises, a pedal that pulsates
*and* has gotten low or soft, or any bearing hum: stop driving on trust.
Brakes and bearings are the two systems where "next month" is the wrong
answer.

So: steering-wheel shake says front, seat shudder says rear, and nine times
out of ten the villain is a rotor face that's no longer even — usually from
pad deposits, rust, or a lazy caliper. Now you know what your car was
saying.
